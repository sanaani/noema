Eleven thousand theorems, on a globe that is almost lying to you

Every Mathlib theorem in the phase 2 corpus is a point on a sphere with 1,472 axes: the average direction of its proof states, as read by a proof-state encoder. Nobody can look at that. This page flattens it onto an ordinary globe two different ways, and each way throws away something different. Turn the object, switch between the two, and read the figures that say how much each one lies.

Read this before trusting the picture

This is a compressed object. In the real space almost every pair of theorems sits close to 90° apart, and no globe can hold that. Click any point and its five true nearest neighbours light up in blue: on one globe they scatter, on the other they cluster, and neither is the truth. Use the page to build intuition for the shape: how many clumps there are, which areas overlap, where the 2026 connections run. Never read a distance off it.

What you are looking at

  • One point per theorem: 11,489 of them, from 335 Mathlib files drawn by a fixed random seed, not chosen by hand. Each is the centroid of that theorem's captured proof states, encoded by the pinned ReProver retriever.
  • Statement-only points. A proof that runs no tactic leaves no states, so its point is the encoder's reading of the theorem statement alone. That is 59% of this corpus. The "proof states vs statement only" colouring separates them; the two kinds are scored apart in the write-up.
  • Arcs are pairs that a theorem written between July 2024 and September 2026 cites together. amber arcs are ordinary positives; pink arcs join theorems from different Mathlib areas that share under 5% of their state vocabulary, the pairs no text-matching method can rank.
  • The arc slider filters on the true angle between the two centroids in 1,472 dimensions, never on how far apart they look here.

How the flattening was made, and measured

  • Global axes. Centre the cloud, take its three principal axes (PCA), project every centroid onto them, scale each back to unit length. Linear, deterministic, no fitting to any label. It shows which directions the corpus spreads along most, and almost nothing about who is near whom.
  • Neighbourhood layout. Start from that globe, then for 300 rounds pull every theorem along the surface toward its ten true nearest neighbours and push it away from random others, renormalising each round. This is the objective UMAP and LargeVis optimise, done on the sphere. It keeps clusters and neighbourhoods; the position of a cluster on the globe and the gaps between clusters carry no information.
  • Variance carried (PCA only) is the share of the cloud's spread the three axes hold; the rest is invisible.
  • Rank agreement is the Spearman correlation between the true angle and the globe angle over 300,000 random pairs. At 1.0 the globe would order every pair correctly.
  • Neighbours kept: of each theorem's ten nearest in the real space, how many are still among its ten nearest on the globe, averaged.
  • On the PCA globe, a point near the centre of the cloud projects to almost nothing, and pushing it out to the surface amplifies noise. The "how much of the point the 3 PCA axes keep" colouring shows where that happened.
